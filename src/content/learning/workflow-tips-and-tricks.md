In my article, ["How to Learn Astrophotography,"](/learning/how-to-learn-astrophotography/) I spend some time discussing several approaches to learning the hobby. The first of those approaches is a pure "tutorial" approach, where the learner piggybacks atop the methods of a astrophotographer who's already established themselves in the hobby and follows a workflow known to produce results.

As such, it's the typical way that people begin a hobby. Because of this, it's a question I get all the time...

***"What is your workflow and why?"***

Answering this question is more complex than what you might imagine. This is because I have several routines, workflows, and events that I try to follow within my own practice that take place in several arenas of the hobby.

Typically, for the most generic of astroimagers, such a workflow might exist within two areas of the hobby...a workflow for ***acquiring data*** and one for ***processing data***.

Certainly, as with all step-by-step methods, such approaches can work to produce good data and nice images; however, while there are some rules of thumb when it comes to developing any workflow, there isn't really any overall correct way of going about it.

To me, there are two problems with such a simplification. The first is that beginners can trap themselves into a particular way of doing something, never really understanding that workflows are living and breathing and evolving processes that truly become more powerful and effective through deeper understanding and customization and personalization. Nor do they have the capability of reacting to events that require them to do something outside what is typical. They lack troubleshooting skills. They do not see the need to learn the theory of why we do what we do.

As such, there are many ways we must learn to augment a basic workflow to get the best results. Likewise, there are often ways to eliminate unnecessary steps, particularly if, as a beginner, we know not WHY we are performing those steps in the first place.

While this can be subject to many factors, such as differing equipment, software choices, locations, and interests, I can certainly detail some of the ways I've tried to refine my own ever-evolving workflows when it comes to data acquisition and processing.

The second problem I see with many who do not stray from a tutorial approach is that they might not be aware that there are other areas of the hobby where a workflow becomes valuable, beyond the areas that deal only in "data."

In this article, while I will be focusing on the various workflows as they apply, we will be targeting some tips and tricks that you might find useful within several areas of the hobby, not just when collecting data or processing an image. I want to consider important some other areas where a process is necessary, such as when maintaining your equipment or when studying imaging or when pushing the boundaries with new skills.

In fact, I see five specific areas when we typically practice our hobby, five distinct "laboratories" where we test out or experiment with new techniques to improve our skills and results. Thus, to define how I create workflows and practice my hobby, I will divide our discussion up into these five LABORATORIES, explaining the "lab rules," major learning objectives made possible within each lab, and warnings against bad practices to watch out for.

## LABORATORY 1: UNDER THE STARS

I like to think of three types of people in this hobby: the ***Imagers***, the ***Astronomers***, and the ***Pretenders***.

So what do I mean? Let's talk about each group and discuss some practice tips for when you are under the stars.

***The Imagers***

Typically, if you fall into this class of people, you likely have a photography background. You might even have a good DSLR camera that you want to put on the back of a telescope. While you have an interest in astronomy, let's be honest, it's all about getting good images as far as you're concerned!

As somebody driven hard to getting good images myself, there have been times when nothing else really mattered - I was going to focus solely on my growth as an ***Imager***. This desire consumed my every day life, where I thought, read, studied, and dreamed about imaging as if my life depended on it. And typically, this is likely going to be a large part of how you do it as well - there should be some desire (and necessity) to read and study even when you are not outdoors acquiring all that data.

But once you get outdoors, you will want to have a solid plan for what to do when the skies are good and bad.

When the skies are good, an ***Imager*** be collecting data. But this is when you need to consider practicing the things that ASSURE good data. I have already written a pretty decent article on that topic, ["Best Data Acquisition Practices,"](/learning/best-data-acquisition-practices/) so I won't repeat that content here; however, I can summarize it by saying that there are certain practices that impact an image more than others.

In the above article, I divide these into three "orders" of importance. To speed up your positive results, you will want to concentrate on those items I've listed as "1st Order Practices," which are important regardless of the type of imaging you do. They include nightly activities such as focusing, collimation, polar alignment, scope/filter selection and dithering. What you decide to do about the wind and seeing quality are "1st Order" critical as well (these are "practices" because you should learn what to do in response to those conditions).

Logically, it means that achieving great data should be considered the end result of good practice. Our stars are sharp because we've worked hard on getting good focus with our collimated optics...and they STAY sharp because we know that the focus will likely shift as the temperature drops over the course of the night. "Faster" scopes made of aluminum, like my FSQ-106 at f/5, need to be refocused every 30 to 45 minutes.

Stars are not trailed because we've aligned our scopes correctly and because we've chosen the proper optics for the exposure lengths we hope to get.

When the wind blows, do we know where to setup our scopes or which optics to use? When the seeing is poor, are we going to still use our big SCT or will we realize that it might be a better night for piggybacking camera lenses or using the rich-field refractor?

To be honest, it's hard to learn imaging when we make bad choices to begin with, so plan your imaging session well...you will want to work smarter, not harder. For example, if you think you will get good results with a 2000mm focal length scope in 5" FWHM seeing, then you really need to work on your perspective regarding what's possible in this hobby. You should spend a little time measuring the seeing quality and understanding what metrics like FWHM and Half-Flux Diameter mean.

For those more interesting in nightscapes and time-lapses, have you researched the optimal ISO settings for your camera? Have you paid attention to how aberrant your lens is when opened up fully and do you know how much better the image can be if you stop down the lenses a little bit? Do you know things like the "Rule of 500" (google it) and do you know how to use it to get the right exposure times? Do you know how to focus the lens? Hint: You can't just set it to the infinity mark - it doesn't work that way.

And what about the bad nights, when the clouds come? I mentioned earlier that you should have a plan for that too!

For those with a CCD camera, cloudy nights are perfect nights to take dark frames.

Is it totally overcast when the sun sets? Then shoot some sky-flats! Or, just take the time to refine your flat-taking process, whatever illumination source you use.

Have you checked polar alignment recently on your permanent observatory scope? Could it have drifted? Even now, it's likely that enough stars are in the sky to refine your alignment using a program like PEMPro. Similarly, if you are using a T-Point model, can you use the time to add a new calibration run?

And because much or our acquisition software does have a sizable learning curve, why not just spend some time looking through the sucker holes in the clouds and play with that software? Maybe you want to experiment with some new scripting/automation software? Use those partly cloudy nights to do that!

Also, being an ***Imager*** under pristine conditions...why are you only running one setup? In the least, have a DSLR on a tripod somewhere nearby running a time-lapse through the evening, perhaps capturing yourself in the frame as you are imaging DSOs through your main scope. My good friend, John Bozeman, has been known to have 3 or 4 imaging setups running simultaneously. Others, like Rogelio Andreo Bernal or Sara Wager have twin refractors/cameras on the same mount for double the efficiency on those great nights. Sure, all that gear adds up, but you'll get their eventually - as somebody who eats and sleeps imaging, dreaming of apochromatic refractors, having more than one scope for imaging is usually quite inevitable!

Finally, and much beyond the scope of this article, a good practice to follow regards optimizing your individual exposures to the "sky limit." What this means, in practice, is that there is a certain length of exposure that is optimal for your sub-exposures, where you can be assured that camera read-noise will have the most minimal contribution to the final image. There are CCD Exposure Calculators out there that teaches you how to find that, but in the meantime, you should know that going too short with your sub-exposures is not optimal for best signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and it's always best to err on the side of your exposures being too long.

In practice, this level isn't too hard to find...I would simply image long enough to where the brightest stars in the image are not quite at their peak saturation value. This will allow you to retain the color in your stars while assuring that you aren't adding more sub-exposures to the overall image "stack" than is necessary where you would suffer a camera read noise hit.

***The Astronomers***

Like most adolescents, I grew up not knowing much about anything beyond 100 feet of me. The exception was when I looked up into the sky and was filled with a sense of wonder. Not quite capable of understanding concepts like the infinitely large, the speed of light, or even what a "light year" was, it took me a little bit of age and maturity before I could really dig into what "space" means. So, at 28, when Hale-Bopp came, I knew that it was time to begin answering some questions about space myself.

And while you could also classify me as an ***Imager***, since photography has always been another fascination of mine, it's the ***Astronomer*** in me that defines what is truly a night of "acceptable practice." I can't tell you how many fruitless nights I've had under the stars as an Imager, but as an Astronomer those same nights would almost always be a success. So, above and beyond the types of practice I talked about as an Imager above, you will actually find the Astronomer in you more fulfilled on any given night since you probably will have much more to take away from the experience.

When I am in "Astronomer mode," I typically have an observing setup to go along with my imaging setup, which I always have since there are only so many clear, good "seeing" nights where I can image - bad seeing constrains me much more as an Imager than as an observer so you can believe that I'll have an imaging scope running. But you might be surprised to know that even if I ONLY have an imaging setup running, there are times when I stop with the 15 minute sub-exposures and my inner ***Astronomer*** completely takes over. I just might decide to go from object to object, taking focus frames or short images, only to remind myself how the object "looks" tonight.

"Hello, Mr. Pleiades, it's been a long time!"

More importantly, and something that is very underrated by most, is that an astronomer can actually talk about his or her image. For example, knowing something about globular clusters enhances your presentation of the image because you can characterize it as the [cosmic rebel](/learning/globular-clusters/) that it is. And because our awesome cameras and dark skies brings all sorts of object catalogs into play as viable targets, being an astronomer allows you to appreciate just how remarkable an image of a "Sharpless" catalog object really is.

Not only that, understanding what galaxies actually are, as well as their types, can lead you to a deeper understanding of particular aspects of your image and introduce new processing goals where such features are concerned, such as processing galaxies for their ejecta or enhancing dust at the galactic core.

But the major difference in the way I practice while under the stars, being also an ***Astronomer***, is that I typically will prescribe for myself an astronomy objective(s) to go along with my imaging objective(s). For example, being that I first learned to "star-hop" around the night sky, I "learned the sky" long before I used a "GOTO" telescope. But because all we ever do as Imagers is click on an target in our planetarium software and then hit the "slew" button, it's really easy to forget where everything is.

So, on any given night, I will make it my objective to be active while the camera is ticking away, being sure to regain familiarity with the location of the planets, using binoculars to refresh my memory on where to find specific deep sky objects, or even pulling out an eyepiece (gasp!) to take a quick visual of an object through another telescope. Not only will this help you regain familiarity with the night sky, but it also allows you to highlight the night sky when you are with others, helping others in their own astronomy learning.

But perhaps, most critically, there's another big reason to be an astronomer which can become a standard part of your imaging practice. ***I can look at the sky visually, whether through the telescope or naked eye, and pretty much tell you the quality of the sky from both the standpoint of transparency and seeing.*** For example, I know the limiting magnitude of the stars in many asterisms and can tell you how dark the sky really is. I can also know what scope to use while I am imaging because I will have gained some familiar visual information about the atmosphere's stability just by looking up. See ***SIDEBAR: Using My Astronomy to Be a Better Imager*** for some real examples.

***The Pretenders***

I think there are a lot of people in the hobby for the wrong reasons. These are the ***Pretenders***. These include people who want others to think they worked harder than they did, are better than they are, and who really aren't interested in learning much of anything. These agendas have no place in the hobby.

Because here's the thing...historically, people come into this hobby for only a few reasons, which is typically either because they love astronomy, they love photography, or they love the challenge. Any of the three REQUIRES you to spend time under the stars.

And at risk of alienating a lot of readers, let me suggest that if you've never spent a single night under the glorious night sky... or if you skipped the whole "buy a telescope" and "collecting your own data" stages...or if you decided to jump straight away into purchasing telescope time on an already established scope, then you are a ***Pretender*** as well. Worse, if you post all of your brilliant images on Facebook to make people think you are a great astronomer, then, well...you likely know how I feel about you.

Now, let's be clear about what I'm NOT saying. I'm not saying you can't buy telescope time or process other people's data and post it to the Internet! For many, often because of age or time constraints - or because it's a part of their prescribed practice - they simply find that the most optimum use of time is to do Astronomy - which is a priority for such people - without waiting around for good sky conditions that might never come. And inevitably, many of us eventually get tired of lugging our equipment outside all the time, so taking the next step of building our own observatory is a logical next step in our evolution as an ***Imager***. And yet others, who've already done it with their own equipment, might come to realize that paying for time on actual, working observatory scopes might even be cheaper than the ***per image cost*** inherent in their own expensive equipment.

But here's the thing...it's important to pay your dues in this hobby. If learning is secondary to fast results (or your ego), then I can't provide any pointers that matter. In fact, this entire article is pointless for you. So, stop reading now (you probably didn't make it this far anyway), give away your equipment to poor astronomers who will actually learn to use it, and drop out all of those Facebook groups where you've been boasting about how great you are.

## LABORATORY 2 - IN THE DARKROOM

Back in old days of film, many of us had a darkroom, where we could process the film ourselves and control the end result of our images. For me, it was only a black & white darkroom, but it was fun, educational, and wonderfully challenging.

Today, the darkroom has been replaced with digital software, even if the end goal of producing a nice image is still the same. But unlike the film darkroom, THIS darkroom takes a lot longer to learn how to use. Generally, we should consider any workflow to be broken down into the following six parts: calibration, registration, integration, channel combining, post-processing, and publishing. Each of these areas deserves its own article (or textbook) and I'll start working on doing that here at All About Astro. But here, let's talk about some of the highlights as you work through these stages of the image, what I consider important practices as a part of your concentrated effort to get better at your skills in the "digital darkroom."

## Sidebar:  PixInsight vs. Photoshop

The entire process of digital image processing has replaced what we would have done with a film image. But unlike sending the film in to process our prints, we have to create our final images ourselves. This represents a massive learning curve for the imager.

While cost plays a large role into our choice of software tools in the digital darkroom, I find that balancing time constraints with the learning of these tools to be the real bottleneck. So, we are left with trying to balance the power that we know these packages can deliver with the practice aspects of time (and cost).

Programs like PixInsight can handle every part of our digital data, from start to finish. Other programs, like Adobe Photoshop, have the ability to produce wonderful images as long as we pair the calibration, registration, and integration steps using another piece of software, like MaxIm, CCDStack, or DeepSkyStacker (DSS).

While PixInsight has been blasted because of its difficult learning curve, it's not like learning Photoshop + MaxIm is any easier. So, if you are on the fence about learning PixInsight, which is actually very reasonably priced, then perhaps you should jump down. Alternatively, there's no reason to use PixInsight (at least at present time) if you already know Photoshop and have gotten proficient using something like DSS for all your "stacking" needs.

While I believe that intimate knowledge of PixInsight can ultimately make for better images in the long run, I think that it will take a LONG while before you will be able to use it at a level of proficiency that exceeds what Photoshop delivers. For many of us, Photoshop is much more intuitive, especially with its use of layers and very user-friendly naming of its tools. And while Photoshop is criticized because you have fewer global controls in the way you select parts of your image (like for a mask), I feel it's much easier than fiddling with PixInsight settings.

And you need to know there is always ***power in simplicity***.

So, as a point of darkroom practice, it's probably a good idea to become proficient in both..or at least its a worthwhile goal. At first, I usually suggest getting a copy of ***Photoshop CS2***, which can often be had for free (or at very little cost). CS2 actually does everything you need it to do for 16-bit astrophotos, so I'd start there.

After a while, if you feel you are missing out on PixInsight, then add it to your tool set, but don't completely switch over all at once. Continue using your old tools and bring in PixInsight for specific processes, especially ones that complement Photoshop well. For me, those would be mostly the non-linear tools in PixInsight, such as DynamicBackgroundExtraction (DBE) and MultiscaleLinearTransform (MLT). I also find the SCNR and HDRMultiscaleTransform processes very useful tools outside of my Photoshop workflow.

Whatever your choice, getting better takes practice!

***Calibration***

When we talk about calibration of a CCD or CMOS chip, we typically mean applying flat fields, dark frames, and bias frames to a data set. But really, the goal of calibration is to make the sensor and the optics function predictably and uniformly across the entire surface of the imaging chip, from pixel to pixel. Thus, the end result of a good calibration is to make the sensor and optics work more toward the IDEAL equipment that we would hope.

For ***Flat-Fields*** (or Flats), it's helpful to know that they perform two helpful functions for us.

First, not all pixels on the chip have the same gain, even if the gain of the entire chip is set at some particular value. If we think of pixels like buckets, it seems likely that not all the buckets are perfectly shaped in comparison to each other, whereas some might catch slightly more rain than others. This can show when neighboring pixels that we think SHOULD have the same value, do not after a certain amount of time because the rate in which they collect photons are slightly different

Second, because there's a variance in the transmission of our optics - dust specks, dust shadowing, and vignetting - these inefficiencies serve to affect "gain" in much the same way, and can therefore be modeled and "flattened" in exactly the same way individual pixel gain can be calibrated.

In other words, "flat-fielding" is an act that CALIBRATES the imaging chip and the optics to assure that everything that SHOULD be transmitting and collecting photons at identical rates TRULY are.

In practice, this is accomplished by taking a picture of an evenly illuminated source and then mathematically ***dividing*** that image into our light frames. Why and how it is divided is best left for another time; however, this knowledge should be useful in many ways, the least of which is when you setup your software to perform the actual calibration.

How you acquire your flats is also far beyond this *general survey* view of them, but how you handle them in the digital darkroom will be consistent across any software platform you use.

I typically retake flat fields when convenient, if I see that my dust patterns have changed somewhat over time enough to effect the image, or if I know that I am dealing with a critical data set. In other words, I obsess over them much less that you do.

At some point, you do need to obsess over these questions. Do I need a master flat frame? Do I need to calibrate my flats? What software settings should I use to assure that the entire calibration routine is working correctly?

While I cannot answer those questions here, I will say that I typically use only ***one single, well imaged flat field***. I use bias frames to allow PixInsight to scale my darks (which I never take to match my flats specifically) to match my single flat field frame. For filtered images, I do take separate flats through each filter...and I do take separate flats when I "bin" my data.

Your ability to take good master flats should play directly into how you decide to use them. For example, "sky flats" are my primary method, but I don't like the lack of consistency I see when I attempt to take enough images at the correct exposure lengths to make good masters for all my filters and binning configurations. Therefore, using one GOOD flat is better than trying to get good master flats in my case. This can and will change once I finally get a large electro-luminescent (EL) panel that can work for all of my scopes.

*While these are primarily acquisition concerns (Laboratory 1), they are important to the decisions we make in the digital darkroom because we just might find ourselves laboring and practicing things that are either **superfluous** or **counter-productive**.*

Like flat-fields, ***Dark frames*** (or just "darks") and ***Bias Frames*** are part of the calibration stage.

Darks are an exposure(s) taken with the lens cap on (totally black) of the same duration and temperature of the light frames. These are necessary because pixel-by-pixel gain also changes with temperature and exposure duration. Thankfully, these changes are VERY predictable, meaning that if you can take an image of total "darkness," then the result will be a "perfect picture" of this thermal signal (thermal noise is something different).

Bias Frames are 0 second (as close as you can get) exposure(s) using the same conditions as your darks. They represent an instantaneous snapshot of the charges held in the pixels prior to the camera exposure accumulating thermal signal. Capturing this is necessary because all flat fields MUST be subtracted of these initial pixel values (called normalization) before they can be accurately divided into your light frames. As such, flats must be bias subtracted (in the least), to properly flatten the field of your image.

Because darks and biases both have camera read-noise and possibly cosmic ray hits, "dark master" and "bias master" frames should be created in order to do good calibration of the image. This can be accomplished by taking 10 or more images (more is better) and stacking them using a rejection algorithm, such as median, sigma, or min/max clipping of the frames. As such, random read noise is eventually averaged out and vastly differing outliers, like cosmic rays, are removed entirely.

To get better with darks and biases, you absolutely need to know why they are needed, what equipment you are using, and how they work within your software settings. While much of this can be handled by tutorial workflows you might find on line, much like flat fields, it's easy to make assumptions that they are always necessary for an image.

But the nice thing is that all you have to do is ***subtract*** that dark frame mathematically from the light frames to rid yourself of these thermal fluctuations of signal.

There is a lot to concern ourselves with here, especially in terms of how we handle the data in processing.

- You see that elephant over there...the one in the room? Yep, it's a DSLR and I'm sure you are wondering what I think about shooting darks with it? Naturally, DSLRs do not come stock with a cooler. So, I ask you, knowing the purpose of a dark frame...do you think darks are going to help? Think it through. What makes them work with TEC cameras is that the entire duration of the exposure is locked into place at your set-point temperature. This is true for BOTH the light frame image and the dark frame. Because a DSLR can't be certain of that, especially since the camera's own electronics greatly heat up during the course of long exposures, then you aren't going to get good modeling of that thermal data. ***Consequently, I don't do dark frames with DSLR cameras.*** Now, if you think it works, then by all means keep at it; however, you really need to know for sure if it's working, which is something we can talk about in the "How to Access" section of the article.

This brings about two important aspects related to your learning...

1.) Your acquisition determines your darkroom routine.

2.) You must test.
