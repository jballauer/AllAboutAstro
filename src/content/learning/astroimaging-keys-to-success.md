No doubt that you've made good progress in being able to take good pictures of the cosmos. You know a lot about what it takes, the techniques involved, and have encountered a lot of the obstacles that stand in your way to getting better at this hobby. But if you are like me, everything you first learned became a jumbled-up mess of disorganized tasks — a "to-do list" of disconnected ideas that you may or may NOT have remembered to do.

So, how can we simplify the process? Better yet, how might we better organize or systematize our tasks in a way that makes sense to us and gives us a road-map to success?

What follows are what I believe to be the over-arching, central ideas of our hobby... the keys to success.

### KEY #1 — Reduce the Variables

Telescopes and cameras and celestial motion and weather and data processing — it all comprises a complex system, with diverse elements all working together to complicate and frustrate. Not only is there a substantial learning curve to accomplish our goal of taking good celestial images, but there seems to be a never-ending stream of unanticipated items that pop up. I think many of us can live with the surprise clouds that overtake the sky during a sequence of images or even the bad seeing moments that wreck a sub-exposure. Heck, I think we can even forgive ourselves of getting bad focus on an occasional image.

But what you should NOT permit are the truly controllable factors that can produce bad results — cable snags, routinely awful focusing, lazy polar alignment, poor collimation, and failure to cool down the telescope — just to name several. Poor practices equate to poor results!

Importantly, the good practices you have learned (see my article called ["Best Data Acquisition Practices"](/learning/best-data-acquisition-practices)) are designed to do ONE thing for you... to reduce the variables that can yield bad results. Because here is the thing... if you have a TON of problems in an image coming from multiple sources of error, then how in the world will you ever be able to figure out what is causing your images to look bad? If you know there is only one issue, then that issue will be fixable. But how can you possibly troubleshoot your images if you permit multiple aspects of the entire system to yield errors? *Truly, you will not be able to tell where one problem starts and another problem starts.*

So, the first key to success is to *reduce the variables* that can cause bad images. For me, I have figured out 4 ways to do this:

- *Keep it simple, stupid!* — Certainly, you've heard of the KISS principle before, and it's never been more helpful than when doing astrophotography. In many cases, we try to over-complicate everything, thinking that we MUST take dark frames and flat-fields and autoguide and dither and program PEC. Slow down; learn one thing at a time; be methodical. Seek to understand how the "single thing" affects your results.
- *Get the right equipment* — Even on a budget, you can greatly increase your success rate by understanding how the right gear can yield positive results. Don't make careless purchases. As a good rule of thumb, look around the Internet at what people are having success with. Seek to duplicate what they are doing. Also, don't cheap out... if you need to wait to save money on a telescope, then that's always the smart move. Using the right equipment can greatly eliminate the number of possible factors that can yield negative results.
- *Don't take shortcuts* — If you adhere to the good practices you have learned, then you will be in good shape to succeed. However, admittedly there are times when I'm not as vigilant in this regard. I might NOT want to re-focus during an image when I KNOW I need to. Or, I might just plop down the telescope and HOPE the autoguider fixes the drift-induced errors. "Hope" is a bad thing when it comes to both astro-imaging AND the financial markets. Don't take shortcuts... turn the uncertain into the certain.
- *Judge your data, not your results* — When you begin in this hobby, you will not know good data from the bad. You must realize this when you attempt to quantify your acquisition success. Beginners can make bad pictures out of perfectly good data, so learn which is which. You can accomplish this by routinely practicing your processing skills on KNOWN good data. This way, when you process your own, you will quickly learn good from bad in terms of data. Secondly, make use of short focus frames and test shots while you are acquiring the data. This not only verifies the potential of a given night, but also assures that you are making the best of it. Don't just trust that your data will be good... verify this before you rip off a 5 hour exposure sequence plus 5 more hours in trying to process something from it.

By employing the above strategies, you increase the likelihood that all that data waiting for you in the morning will actually be usable. This raises your success rate and makes the actual data processing much easier.

### KEY #2 — Know Your Equipment

One of the fortunate aspects of my "career" in this hobby is that I have managed to find myself in situations where I get to use a TON of telescope gear. I have gained a really solid perspective on what a certain scope, mount, or camera SHOULD do in any given situation and I know what to expect from the equipment I am using at any given time, all because I can relate each experience with something else.

As the average user, however, you probably don't have that luxury. How do you know what results you can expect if you buy Scope A over Scope B? Do you have a perspective of how much more difficult imaging at 2500mm in focal length actually is if you have never imaged with a SCT? Do you know what to expect when you use a new autofocuser in your image train, having never really used one before?

While having a vast perspective on using such a diversity of telescope gear is a luxury — it's not a requirement — you should realize that you can gain a similar perspective if you find success with a given setup and devote quality time to perfecting your results. In truth, the better your images become, the more you will have learned what bad data looks like as you've worked hard to improve gear performance. Unfortunately, many amateurs might give up too quickly on a telescope, or they look too quickly to "upgrade" without really achieving the full potential of their current gear.

Moreover, using your equipment in a systematic way can pave the way to faster successes. For example, is there a certain way you should orient your camera? My default camera orientation places north and south to the top and bottom of the chip respectively. This matches the hand controller keys of the typical electronic telescope. This means that east and west will run side to side on the chip. Practically speaking, this should help putting objects onto the chip much faster. But the real reason to do this is so that you can understand the components to bad seeing, tracking, and polar alignment. For example, because right ascension moves east and west on the chip, then every error related to it will show up side to side. Are stars elongated east-to-west? Then you either have a tracking or guiding error. Are stars elongated diagonally? If so, then it's drift due to polar alignment.

One of the more difficult things, especially for beginners, is determining a guiding solution. If you auto-guide, do you know where the star field is relative to your imaging field? Can you find guide stars sufficient enough in brightness, and if not, do you know where you can find them? What price is there to be paid when using an off-axis guider vs. a separate guide scope... are you willing to pay that price? What problems might a guide-scope solution cause?

And then there is your mount! We all know that it's the most important part of the imaging equation, but how does that show itself in practice? Vibration is a large source of issues, particularly with less expensive mounts in windy conditions. Not that it's impossible to get good images with such mounts, but the user can save some headaches by making sure that payloads are minimal to moderate, the telescope has less of a wind-profile, cables are properly managed, the mount is properly balanced, and periodic error curves are properly applied (if the mount has PEC capabilities).

As far as the optics are concerned, there are several aspects during imaging to be familiar with. Most importantly, it's critically important to be familiar with what good focus looks like in a particular instrument... and more importantly, being familiar with how focus can change over the night as the temperature varies. Scopes with a narrow zone of focus ("fast" focal ratios) will be more likely to shift focus during an imaging run. Likewise, scopes constructed with aluminum tubes will contract a surprisingly large amount as the temperature drops. In a typical night an instrument like a fast apochromatic refractor can require re-focusing twice an hour. Understanding why and how this happens... and what it looks like when it does... is a very valuable bit of knowledge. Conversely, scopes with longer zones of focus (like "slower" SCTs), might never need to be refocused once proper focus is set. This is especially true with instruments constructed with zero expansion materials like carbon fiber.

And then there is collimation of the optics. For many with refractors, you've likely never given anything a second thought... it's setup and let 'er rip. But with SCTs, RCs, and newts, collimation becomes critical. Even then, an imager needs to look beyond simple collimation (which is typically easy) and make sure that the optics are performing in the correct way. Most setups are quite involved, many of which require specific imaging trains, back-focal distances, and properly spaced adapters. A software tool like CCDInspector (www.ccdware.com) can resolve a lot of issues, like camera tilt and field curvature, but being able to look at an image and knowing if your optics are performing as they should has a lot to say with regard to great images.

Finally, knowing your equipment always means to recognize the importance of image scale (sampling rate) to the combination of camera to telescope. Small refractors, used by many beginning imagers, yield very relaxed image scales, meaning that the image resolution is typically larger than all but the worst of your seeing conditions. It will also offer a buffer to auto-guiding performance and mount tracking accuracy. Most importantly, users of multiple imaging setups (with multiple image scale possibilities) can best choose the scope which delivers the best chance of success on any given night. For example, I seldom take a large focal length telescope (with image scales less than 1"/pixel) to the Texas Star Party because I know that the poor seeing will not provide the details normally afforded by the high resolution setup.

### KEY #3 — Understand the Data

We process data. When you get started, it's difficult to know what you are looking at. Heck, even when you open the FITS file from the astro camera, it's all black. Truly, you are in new territory. A proper stretch is typically the first processing move you MUST get correct, which allows you to see all the data on screen without blowing out the highlight areas. Equally important is the screen stretch, which displays the information you've collected during an imaging run. Doing so allows you to check object orientation and framing, if you have good focus, if the auto guider is behaving, and it lets you evaluate whether or not the object is worth spending time on. In total, if you don't like what you see in a stretched out 10 minute image, then it's very unlikely you will like what you see in a whole stack of them. ## Sidebar: Judging the Screen Stretch

One of the most telling indicators of the would-be quality of an image is to properly stretch and evaluate the first sub-exposure of a sequence. A vast amount of information is revealed:

- Focus quality — Does it appear perfect or is there more tweaking to do?
- Sky quality — Is the seeing causing issues at your chosen exposure length? Should you shorten sub-exposures or perhaps bin the frames for increased SNR?
- Object orientation and framing — Did the GOTO hit correctly and is the frame showing the feature I KNOW I wanted?
- Potential background/shadow features — Are there features I wasn't aware of... something that might be worth spending some extra exposure time on?
- Alignment, tracking, and auto-guiding precision — At my given image scale, is there enough accuracy to make round stars? Do I need to choose a new guide star or refine my guiding parameters?
- Light pollution effects — Does the image truly stand out from the bright sky-background? Should I change my exposure length, choose different filters, or work in a different part of the sky? Is there any ambient light nearby affecting the image?

When it comes to the screen stretch, most important to me is the evaluation of whether the given object or subject is worth it from the standpoint of time and energy? There's typically a reason why everybody with a short refractor ends up shooting the same objects over and over... because they lend themselves enough object signal to get the job done. But in good, dark sky conditions, a good initial screen stretch can let you see if there's something special you might be able to do! Truly, there have been some nights when I felt that no target was outside of my reach!

To get a good screen stretch, your software is all important. From an efficiency standpoint, your image capture software should provide the capability to show an exaggerated screen stretch.
